Why Your Toilet Keeps Running (and When to Stop DIYing It)

A running toilet wastes real water every hour it continues, and in Melbourne, hard water is almost always the reason it started in the first place.

The Usual Culprit

It's Almost Always The Flapper Or Fill Valve

Inside every toilet tank, a flapper seals the drain hole between flushes, and a fill valve controls how the tank refills and shuts off. When a toilet runs continuously, one of these two components has almost always failed to seal or shut off completely. Melbourne's hard water accelerates this specific failure. Mineral scale builds up on the flapper's rubber seal and inside the fill valve mechanism faster than it would in a softer-water market, which is why running toilets show up here more often than the national average would suggest.

Diagnosing It Yourself

A Simple Test Before Calling Anyone

Remove the tank lid and watch what happens after a flush. If the flapper doesn't fully close, or you can see water trickling past it into the bowl, that's your answer, the flapper needs replacing. If the flapper looks fine but water keeps entering the tank and overflowing into the overflow tube, the fill valve is the problem instead.

A food-coloring test can also confirm a flapper leak specifically: add a few drops to the tank water without flushing, then wait 15 minutes. If color appears in the bowl without flushing, the flapper is letting water through.

The DIY Fix

Replacing A Flapper Is A Reasonable DIY Job

A basic flapper replacement is genuinely one of the more approachable DIY plumbing repairs, most hardware stores carry universal replacements for a few dollars, and the job takes fifteen minutes without special tools. Shut off the water at the toilet's own supply valve, flush to drain the tank, unhook the old flapper, and attach the new one following the packaging instructions.

A fill valve replacement is slightly more involved but still within reach for a reasonably handy homeowner, it requires disconnecting the supply line and removing the old valve from inside the tank. Whichever part you're replacing, the connections still need to meet code. Both fall under International Plumbing Code (IPC) requirements incorporated into the Florida Building Code, which is worth keeping in mind if a repair also involves replacing the supply line itself rather than just the internal tank components.

When It's More Than A Simple Swap

Signs You're Past The DIY Point

If you've already replaced the flapper and the toilet still runs, the problem may be a worn or corroded valve seat. That's the surface the flapper actually seals against, which a new flapper alone won't fix. A valve seat can be resurfaced or may require a different flapper design suited to an irregular seal, and diagnosing which one applies isn't always obvious without experience.

A toilet that runs, refills, and runs again in a repeating cycle even with a new flapper and fill valve installed correctly often points to a water pressure issue upstream. That's a different problem requiring a broader look at your home's plumbing rather than another part swap at the toilet itself.

What It's Actually Costing You

Why This Is Worth Fixing Promptly

A continuously running toilet can waste a significant amount of water over the course of a month, all of it showing up on your water bill for no benefit. Unlike a dripping faucet, which is usually visually obvious, a running toilet is easy to miss if it's in a guest bathroom or the sound isn't loud enough to notice from another room, which means it can run for weeks before anyone catches it.

The Hard Water Connection

Why Melbourne Sees This More Than Most Markets

It's worth understanding why this particular failure is so common here specifically. Melbourne's water, drawn from Floridan Aquifer wells and Lake Washington, carries enough dissolved mineral content that scale forms on rubber seals and plastic mechanisms faster than it would in a softer-water city. The flapper's rubber seal is especially susceptible, since scale deposits change its surface texture just enough to prevent a clean seal against the valve seat, even when the flapper itself isn't visibly damaged.

This is also why replacing a flapper with the same generic part sometimes doesn't fully solve the problem long-term, if scale has already etched the valve seat's surface, even a brand-new flapper is sealing against a compromised surface underneath it.

A Quick Cost Comparison

DIY Parts Versus A Service Call

A basic flapper costs very little at any hardware store, and a fill valve isn't much more, which is exactly why attempting the DIY fix first makes sense for most homeowners. Where it stops making financial sense is after two or three part swaps haven't solved the problem. At that point, the cost of parts you've already bought plus the value of your own time spent troubleshooting usually exceeds what a single diagnostic visit would have cost from the start.

One Toilet Or All Of Them

Why This Matters For Multi-Bathroom Homes

If more than one toilet in your home has started running around the same time, that's worth noting specifically. It points toward a household-wide water pressure or hardness issue rather than coincidental part failures in unrelated fixtures. A single toilet running on its own is almost always that specific unit's flapper or fill valve reaching the end of its service life. Multiple toilets acting up together suggests something upstream affecting all of them simultaneously, which is worth mentioning to whoever diagnoses the problem rather than treating each toilet as an isolated case.

The Sound Test

Listening For The Problem Even Without Watching The Tank

Many homeowners first notice a running toilet by sound rather than by opening the tank lid at all, a faint hissing or trickling noise that continues well after the last flush. If you hear this but aren't sure which toilet it's coming from in a multi-bathroom home, put a few drops of food coloring in each tank one at a time and check which bowl picks up color without being flushed. This narrows down the source before you even start troubleshooting the specific component, which saves both time and unnecessary part purchases if the sound is faint or coming from a bathroom you don't use often.
